About The Art of Possibility

In the book, The Art of Possibility, Rosamund Stone Zander, a family systems therapist, and Benjamin Zander, the longtime conductor of the Boston Philharmonic, explore a simple yet powerful idea: the way we see ourselves shapes what we believe is possible.

One of their most well-known practices is called "Giving an A." On the very first day of class, Benjamin would give every student an A, not as a reward for past achievement, but as an invitation to step beyond the fear of failure. He would then ask them to write a letter from the future, describing the person they had already become. Not a wish. Not a goal. A declaration.

At Bajaj Auto, your Self-Development Plan (SDP) is that same declaration. It is an opportunity to reflect on who you aspire to be, the strengths you want to build, and the impact you want to create.

We are giving you the A. Who will you choose to become?

Honest reflection on where you have done well, where you could grow, and where you want to go.

Question 4
"Where I stand today"
Looking back on the past year, what moments best reflect who you are at your best?
  • ›Where did you feel most confident, effective, or at your best this year? What was it about those situations that allowed you to thrive?
  • ›Think about times when you created value, solved a problem, supported someone, or influenced an outcome. What strengths were you drawing on in those moments?
  • ›Think about the moments when others relied on you most. What did they see in you that made them turn to you?
  • ›When were you able to contribute more than was expected of you, and what strengths or qualities made that possible?
We all have areas where we know there was more in us, and that awareness is where growth begins.

Growth Conversation Checklist

1. Before the Conversation

I have set aside quiet time to reflect before this meeting, I am not rushing straight from another task.
I have written down 2-3 specific moments from the past few months where I felt I did my best work.
I have written down 1-2 moments where things did not go as planned, and what I learned from each one.
I have thought honestly about what kind of support I actually need, not just what sounds good to ask for.
I have asked myself: am I going into this wanting to be heard, or wanting to grow?

2. Checking My Blind Spots

I am not only thinking about the last few weeks. I have looked at the full shape of my work.
I have prepared to share context for my goals and reflections, not assume that my manager would know the full extent.
I am actively considering if I am comparing myself to colleagues as I discuss my growth, and not my own goals and previous progress.
I have asked myself honestly if I am downplaying my wins, or glossing over the places I have genuinely struggled.

3. What to Bring Up

I have one clear question I want to ask about where I am headed and I have written it down before the meeting.
I have thought about the skills I want to build, not just the proficiency or level I want to reach.
I have been honest with myself about what has been holding me back, so I can name it out loud.
I have thought about what growth means to me right now, even if it may be different from what my manager assumes.
I have reflected on what is working well in the way my manager and I communicate, and what I would like to see more of going forward.

4. During the Conversation

I have reminded myself to listen more than I speak.
I have given myself permission to say: ‘I would like to think about that before I answer.’
I am open to asking follow-up questions to understand more deeply, not to argue or justify myself.

5. After the Conversation

I have written down the key things that were said, before the clarity fades.
I have identified one concrete action I will take before we next meet.
I have asked myself honestly: did I show up the way I wanted to?
I have noted anything that felt unresolved, so I can bring it up next time.

The LCFW Framework

These four attributes are the lens through which Bajaj Auto thinks about leadership and growth. They are not boxes to tick, they are a way of thinking about who you want to become.

Will
Identity · Ownership · Commitment
Conviction
Knowing what you stand for and holding it, even when the room disagrees.
Integrity
The consistency of who you say you are and how you actually show up.
Commitment
Caring enough to stay with something when it gets hard. Owning it, not just doing it.
Energy
Passion · Inspiration · Entrepreneurial
Passion
An unquenchable enthusiasm to excel and achieve what you believe in.
Inspirational
The ability to motivate and energise the people around you.
Entrepreneurial
Overcoming constraints through unconventional solutions and resourcefulness.
Understanding
Intellect · Meticulousness · Laterality
Intellect
Not just what happened, but why. Going to the root cause, not the surface answer.
Meticulousness
Showing up with the same care and thoroughness whether or not anyone is watching.
Laterality
Seeing beyond your function. Connecting dots across domains, thinking wider than your brief.
Capability
Capacity · Agility · Tenacity
Capacity
Growing into greater complexity and responsibility over time.
Agility
Learning fast and adapting when things change around you.
Tenacity
Staying with something when it gets hard. Persistence in the face of difficulty.

Before you commit to your goals, draw on the sources around you. The most meaningful goals usually surface from patterns that appear across more than one.

💬
Manager Feedback

📋
Assessment Reports
Psychometric reports like Harrison, DISC, or Hogan. What insights do they offer about your preferences and patterns?
🧭
iEXCEL Assessment
If you have completed iEXCEL, revisit your report. Which observations or recommendations still resonate?
🔄
360 Feedback
If you have participated in a 360, review your report. What recurring themes emerge across stakeholders?
🏛️
Development Centre Reports
Revisit your DC report and your Individual Development Plan (IDP). Which priorities are still relevant today?
🗂️
Skill Matrices
If functional or role-based skill matrices exist for your role or BU, check them. Which capabilities matter most?
👥
Feedback from Others
Speak with trusted colleagues, mentors, or stakeholders. An outside-in view often reveals blind spots and opportunities.

As you review these inputs, focus less on individual comments and more on recurring themes. The most meaningful development goals often emerge from patterns that appear across multiple sources.

Sample Goals for Goal Setting

Three goals, three domains

These examples show what well-formed Functional, Behavioural, and Leadership goals can look like. Use them as a reference for shape and depth, not as templates to copy.

Functional
Sample Goal 1
What I want to build
Get properly skilled at root-cause analysis (5-Why / fishbone) for quality deviations, instead of just fixing the symptom and moving on.
Why does this matter
Right now when a deviation comes up, I patch it well enough to get the line running again, but the same issue resurfaces two or three weeks later because nobody actually traced it back to the real cause. I am capable of doing this properly, I just have not built the habit or the method.
I will know I have grown when
A deviation I investigate does not reappear in the next production cycle, and my corrective action notes hold up when QA reviews them.
Action plan
Do · 70%
Apply 5-Why on every deviation I personally handle for the next quarter, even minor ones, and document it properly instead of just logging "resolved" in the handover sheet.
Learn · 10%
Sit with the quality engineer on one real investigation (not a training session) and watch how they push past the first answer to the actual root cause.
Connect · 20%
Ask the shift supervisor who has the best track record on closed-out deviations to walk me through two of their past investigation reports.
Support I need
Relevant courses or certifications, and some time with a mentor who can help me with the same.
Behavioural
Sample Goal 2
What I want to build
Build confidence in speaking up early, especially when I see risks, gaps, or a different point of view.
Why does this matter
There have been situations where I noticed a concern but waited too long to raise it because I wanted to be completely sure first. I want to become more comfortable contributing my perspective earlier, even when my view is still developing. This matters because silence can slow down decisions or prevent the team from seeing a risk in time.
I will know I have grown when
In meetings or project discussions, I am able to respectfully raise concerns, ask clarifying questions, or offer a different perspective without overthinking whether my input is perfect.
Action plan
Do · 70%
In key meetings, I will prepare one question, risk, or point of view in advance and make sure I contribute it during the discussion. When I disagree, I will practise framing it constructively, such as: "One concern I have is..." or "Another way to look at this could be..."
Learn · 10%
Read or complete short learning on difficult conversations, influencing without authority, and assertive communication. I will also reflect after important meetings on where I spoke up and where I held back.
Connect · 20%
Observe colleagues who challenge ideas constructively and ask them how they prepare for difficult conversations. I will also request feedback from my manager on whether my communication is clear, timely, and balanced.
Support I need
Opportunities to present updates or risks in team forums, even if the topic is small initially. I would also value my manager’s coaching before and after difficult stakeholder conversations so I can build confidence through real situations, not just theory.
Leadership
Sample Goal 3
What I want to build
Own the onboarding of new operators on my line end-to-end, instead of just answering their questions when they happen to ask.
Why does this matter
New hires currently pick things up by trial and error and by bothering whoever is free, including me, but nobody actually owns getting them competent. I am one of the people they already come to, so the gap is in taking responsibility for it properly, not in being asked to.
I will know I have grown when
A new operator I have onboarded is running the line independently and correctly within two weeks, without needing to be walked through the same step twice.
Action plan
Do · 70%
Build a simple one-page checklist of what a new operator needs to know in their first two weeks on this line, and personally walk the next new hire through it instead of leaving it to whoever is nearby.
Learn · 10%
Look at how the changeover team structures their cross-training so new people get hands-on practice early instead of just shadowing.
Connect · 20%
Ask the trainer who runs new-hire induction what they have seen work and fail when operators try to onboard people informally on the floor.
Support I need
Time with tenured employees and supervisors to understand what works and fails for activities outside of my domain. Alignment and guidance on expectations, responsibilities, and how progress should be tracked.

Use these as a reference, not a script. Your goals should be yours.
